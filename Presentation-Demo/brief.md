# Brief: Reproducible Research Workshop for Undergraduates

This brief describes one presentation. Standing rules for every deck are in the presentation-guidelines.md file. If this brief and the guidelines conflict, follow the guidelines and point out the conflict.

## This Session
**Title:** Work your Future Self Can Follow: An Introduction to Reproducible Research
**Format:** 50 Minute in-person workshop in a library classroom
**Offered:** Several times per semester; students ign up through Yale University Library's workshop calendar
**Deck Length:** 13-15 slides, including a title slide, a sources slide, and a further-reading slide

## Audience
* First- and second-year undergraduates from any discipline: humanities, social sciences, and sciences
* Most have written research papers. A few have managed data, code, or multi-week projects with multiple files.
* Assume no coding experience and no familiarly with Git or GitHub
* Students attend voluntarily, often because a research project or methods course is coming up. Keep the tone practical and encouraging, not cautionary.

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of the workshop, attendees should be able to..
1. **Define reproducibility** and explain why this matters for your projects 
2. **Describe each principle in the FAIR guidelines** and provide an example
3. **Describe two everyday habits that support reproducibility: **
   consistent file naming and version control. Explain how each
   connects to the FAIR principles, using familiar examples such as
   dated file names and the version history in Google Docs.

## Structure
- **Opening (5 min):** A relatable scenario, such as a student who
  can't make sense of their own project after a semester away.
- **What reproducibility is and why it matters (10 min):** Outcome 1.
- **The FAIR principles (12 min):** Outcome 2.
- **Everyday habits: file naming and version control (10 min):**
  Outcome 3. Present this section as how to put FAIR into practice.
  Show a before-and-after list of file names, and describe version
  history as a concept. No tool demonstrations.
- **Activity, "Could Someone Else Do This?" (10 min)**
- **Debrief, getting help, and wrap-up (3 min)**

## Sources
All content must come from the files in `sources/`. Each type of source has a specific role:
* **The Turing Way exerpts** (TuringWay-*.md) are the main source for teaching. 
* **Intro-to-FAIR.pdf** is a slide deck from a co-taught workshop on FAIR. This can also be used for the teaching elements.
* **Open-access articles** (articles/) provide evidence and examples. Use them to support claims about why reproducibility matters, to give real research examples, or to add a finding or statistic. Every slide that draws on one of the articles cites it by author and year.
* **Images** listed in sources/images/images.md, used as that file describes.

## Avoid
* Jargon without a plain-language definition: "provenance," "metadata," "FAIR," "computational environment"
* Instructions specific to R, Python, or the command line
* Presenting Git or GitHub as required. Mention them as options students may encounter later.
* Framing reproducibility mainly around fraud, retractions, or a "crisis." Lead with the benefits to students' own work.

## Deliverables
1. **outline.md:** One line per slide giving the title, key point, and source file. Stop after writing it and wait for approval.
2. **reproducible-research-workshop.pptx:** Built on library-template.pptx, with speaker notes on every slide. Write the speaker notes as talking points the presenter can say aloud, not as a script.
3. **facilitator-handout.md:** One page for whoever teaches the session, covering session goals, timing, activity setup and sample answers, and two likely student questions with suggested answers.

## Success Looks Like
A staff member who didn't build the deck could teach the session from the slides, speaker notes, and handout alone.
